## Session 1 - Introduction to metagenomics, goals and opportunities

### What is metagenomics?

Metagenomics is the study of genetic material recovered directly from environmental or clinical samples, without the need to isolate and cultivate individual microorganisms. By sequencing **all the DNA (or RNA) present in a sample**, metagenomics gives us access to the composition and functional potential of whole microbial communities — bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses and eukaryotic microbes — in their ecological context.

The term _metagenomics_ was coined in the late 1990s, but the field took off with the advent of high-throughput sequencing: culture-independent approaches suddenly made it possible to explore the so-called **"unculturable majority"** — the estimated >99% of microbial species that have never been grown in pure culture. From the first 16S rRNA gene surveys to today's whole-genome shotgun sequencing, metagenomics has become a cornerstone of microbial ecology, clinical diagnostics, biotechnology and planetary health monitoring.

### Goals and opportunities

Depending on the question at hand, a metagenomics study can pursue different goals:

* **Who is there?** — taxonomic profiling of the community (which organisms, and in which proportions).
* **What can they do?** — functional profiling (which metabolic pathways, enzymes or traits are encoded by the community).
* **Who is doing what?** — combining taxonomy and function, e.g. through metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) or metatranscriptomics.

These questions arise in a wide variety of fields: **environmental** studies (soil, water, extreme environments, biogeochemical cycles), **clinical** applications (gut microbiome, infectious disease, antimicrobial resistance), and **biotechnological** opportunities (enzyme discovery, bioremediation, agriculture, industrial fermentation).

### Study design considerations

Before any sequencing takes place, a good metagenomics study is designed on paper. The most common pitfalls in microbiome research originate in the experimental design rather than in the analysis, so it is worth pausing here:

!!! question "Questions to ask yourself before starting"
    * **Biological question:** which comparison (conditions, time points, gradients) will answer it?
    * **Replication:** how many biological replicates per group? (Under-replicated designs cannot support statistical testing.)
    * **Controls:** negative controls (extraction blanks, PCR blanks to detect reagent contamination) and positive controls (mock communities to assess accuracy).
    * **Confounding variables:** batch effects, extraction kits, sequencing runs, storage time — are they balanced across groups?
    * **Sampling and storage:** how were the samples collected, preserved and extracted? The protocol strongly shapes the resulting community profile.
    * **Sequencing strategy:** targeted (amplicon) or shotgun? Short or long reads? How deep do I need to sequence?

### Sequencing technologies: short and long reads

Sequencing technologies have evolved to provide us with different types of reads. Each type has its own applications and considerations for QC, and the choice of platform influences every subsequent step of the analysis:

* **Short reads**: generated by platforms like Illumina, they are typically 75–300 bp in length. They offer high throughput and very low per-base error rates, making them the workhorse of both amplicon and shotgun metagenomics. Their main limitation is that the reads are much shorter than most microbial genomes and genes, which complicates the reconstruction of complete genomes and of repetitive or strain-variable regions.
* **Long reads**: produced by platforms such as PacBio and Oxford Nanopore, they can be over 10 kb in length (and now routinely beyond 100 kb). They span complete operons and even complete bacterial genomes, which greatly helps assembly and the resolution of strains and plasmids — at the cost of higher per-base error rates (although modern chemistries and consensus methods have largely closed this gap) and, historically, lower throughput.

??? tip "Not either/or"
    Increasingly, studies combine both technologies in **hybrid** assemblies, using short reads for accuracy and long reads for continuity. Throughout this course you will work mainly with Illumina short reads, but the QC principles you learn apply to long reads as well — you will see PacBio data in the practical below.

## Session 2 - Quality control and contamination assessment

### Overview of quality control

Quality control (QC) is a critical step in any NGS workflow, and the metagenomics workflow is no exception. It ensures that the data used for analysis is of high quality and free from errors that could compromise the results. In this session, we will discuss the importance of QC and introduce the tools we will use to assess and improve the quality of our sequencing reads.

Poor read quality, adapter contamination, PCR duplicates or foreign DNA (host, phiX spike-in, reagent contaminants) left in the data will propagate through every downstream step: biased diversity estimates, false taxonomic assignments, fragmented assemblies and unreliable MAGs. QC is thus not a formality but the foundation of every analysis in this course.

### Quality assessment tools

We will use **FastQC** to assess the quality of our reads:

* **FastQC**: A widely used tool for quality checking of raw sequence data coming from high throughput sequencing pipelines.

### Tips: FastQC output explained

!!! tip "Reading the report at a glance"
    Each module is flagged **pass** (green), **warning** (yellow) or **fail** (red). A single warning does not automatically mean the data are bad, meaning that modules must be interpreted together; always compare raw _vs_ cleaned reads. In metagenomics, some deviations (e.g. GC content or duplication) are expected because a sample contains many organisms.

The most relevant modules to look at when assessing the quality of your reads:

| Module | What it shows | What to look for |
|--------|---------------|------------------|
| **Per Sequence Quality Scores** | Distribution of the average quality score over all reads. | A second peak at low quality reveals a subset of poor-quality reads. |
| **Per Base Sequence Content** | Proportion of each base (A, T, C, G) at each position across all reads. | A non-random or strongly biased profile suggests contamination or a library-construction bias. |
| **Per Sequence GC Content** | Observed GC distribution compared with a theoretical distribution. | A shifted or secondary peak can indicate contamination or an unexpected organism. |
| **Per Base N Content** | Proportion of bases called as `N` at each position (undetermined bases). | A high or rising `N` content points to failing base calls and poor quality. |
| **Sequence Length Distribution** | Distribution of read lengths. | Unexpected lengths may reflect trimming problems, degradation or adapter read-through. |
| **Sequence Duplication Levels** | Percentage of reads that are duplicates of other reads. | High duplication suggests PCR amplification bias or low-complexity libraries. |
| **Overrepresented Sequences** | Sequences appearing more often than expected. | Can be adapter, primer, rRNA or a contaminant — or genuinely abundant biological sequence. |
| **Adapter Content** | Presence of adapter sequences along the reads. | Adapter content rising towards the read end means trimming is needed. |
| **Kmer Content** | Kmers (short nucleotide sequences) that are overrepresented at particular positions. | Position-specific anomalies can indicate contamination or biases. |

!!! warning "Some modules are expected to warn in metagenomics"
    Duplication levels, GC content and per-base sequence content can look unusual simply because many genomes are mixed together. Interpret these modules in the context of the sample type, and give more weight to quality scores, adapter content and overrepresented sequences.
